# Polaris: Dynamic Multi-Agent Orchestration for Conversational Enterprise Analytics

[中文](2608.14246.md) | **English** · [Home](../README.en.md) · [Research Library](../library/README.en.md)

[Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14246) · **Importance: 3/5**

> **Research delta.** Polaris assigns enterprise-analytics work across specialized querying, visualization, and reasoning roles through Dynamic Task Coordination. Current evidence shows that the packaged system can complete a controlled case set, but does not isolate the contribution of multi-agent coordination against a simpler controller.

## Problem

Conversational enterprise analytics is not one NL→SQL call. A request may require data identification, query generation, interpretation, visualization, and revisions after intermediate results appear. An end-to-end call mixes task decomposition, tool execution, and cross-step state, making failures difficult to locate.

Polaris asks whether role specialization and online task assignment can organize this multi-stage analytics chain.

## Mechanism

Specialized agents take querying, visualization, and reasoning responsibilities. Dynamic Task Coordination assigns work according to the current task and intermediate state.

The control flow can be summarized as:

`user request → decompose/assign → specialized tool work → coordinate intermediate outputs → compose analytic response`

This mechanism expands orchestration capacity. Using multiple agents, however, is not by itself an attribution result.

## Closest comparison

The decisive missing matched control would hold the base model, tools, prompts, context, and total budget constant while merging the roles into one controller, or disabling Dynamic Task Coordination in favor of fixed routing.

The paper reports no such single-agent, DTC-off, or component control. Results therefore cannot distinguish role decomposition from extra model calls, tool configuration, prompt scaffolding, or dynamic coordination itself.

## Decisive evidence

Tables 1–2 report **40 synthetic Airbnb cases**. The three metrics are strictly **retrieved-context semantic similarity, context precision, and final-response answer relevancy**, scoring **0.85 / 0.99 / 0.90**; under the paper's thresholds, their pass rates are **100% / 100% / 92.5%**.

These results support only a bounded conclusion: the complete Polaris package reaches the paper's reported thresholds for retrieved context and final-response relevance on this synthetic set. They do not measure visualization quality and are not causal evidence that a multi-agent design outperforms a single-agent design.

## What remains unproven

- There is no matched single-agent baseline, DTC-off comparison, or role/component ablation.
- Extra calls, tokens, latency, and coordination overhead are not included in a cost-matched comparison.
- Forty synthetic Airbnb cases do not establish behavior under real enterprise schemas, permissions, dirty data, business-definition drift, or evolving multi-turn requests.
- Recovery after failure and propagation of incorrect intermediate outputs are not evaluated systematically.
- There is no causal validation, and the paper supplies **no code/reproduction link** for reproducing the experiment or checking component-level contributions from the manuscript itself.

## Field-map consequence

Polaris enters **Planning & Semantic Interaction** as an `early_signal`. It suggests that the Data Agent research object is expanding from “generate one query” to “plan and coordinate a multi-role analytics workflow”; Execution & Transformation remains a downstream boundary that later evidence must separate.

It does not yet change the stable field map or support a claim that multi-agent systems are superior. The next evidence should include a matched controller, a DTC-off ablation, total cost, failure recovery, and more realistic enterprise tasks.
